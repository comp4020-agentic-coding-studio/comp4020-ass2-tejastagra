The subject line is where the triage decision usually gets made, which means
it carries more of the email's argument than writers give it credit for. A
subject line that describes the email ("Following up", "Quick question") asks
the reader to open it to find out what it's for. A subject line that states
the specific, checkable thing inside lets the reader triage correctly without
opening anything — which, for the emails that shouldn't be opened, is a
courtesy, not a loss.

The test is whether the subject line survives being read on its own,
stripped of the email underneath it. "Introduction" and "Following up"
fail immediately — they describe a category of email, not this one. "Grant
deadline moved to Friday" passes, because a reader could act correctly on
it without ever opening the message. Most writers know this in the
abstract and still write descriptive subject lines, because the specific
fact usually lives three sentences into the body, and moving it up feels
like giving away the pitch before it's made. It isn't a pitch. It's the
one piece of information the reader needs to triage correctly, and burying
it costs the sender more than showing it ever would.

"Survives on its own" means something specific: a reader can decide two
things from the subject line alone — whether this concerns them at all,
and whether it needs acting on now or can wait. A descriptive subject line
answers neither. The reader is left with an unresolved guess, and the only
way to resolve a guess is to open the message, which is the exact cost the
subject line was supposed to spare them. Ambiguity doesn't just weaken a
subject line; it forces the open it was meant to make unnecessary.

> Subject: Following up

A redacted example, sent as a second attempt after silence. It restates
that a first email existed without adding anything the reader can act on
— not what it's about, not why it's relevant now, not what happens if they
don't reply. "Following up: still looking for 10 minutes on the Q2 pricing
proposal before Friday" answers all three; "Following up" alone answers
none of them, so the reader has no way to triage it except by opening it.

It's also worth noting how common the failure is. Hunter's analysis of 31
million emails found "Quick question" — a subject line with the identical
problem — opened at 28.7%, against a 32.9% average across all subject
lines: below baseline, from the single most reached-for choice in the
language.

## Exercise

Take five subject lines from real cold emails you've received or sent.
Rewrite each so a reader could correctly triage it without opening the
message, and note what information you had to move up from the body to do
that.
