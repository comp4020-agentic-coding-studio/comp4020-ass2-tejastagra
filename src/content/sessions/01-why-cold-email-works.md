Most cold email gets deleted unread, which is a reasonable response to most
cold email. This week starts from the exceptions instead: emails that got
read, and got a reply, from people who owed the sender nothing.

Dhruv Ghulati's email to Mark Cuban about Factmata is one. Cuban invested
$500,000 off it. Nothing about the pitch was charming — a plain problem
statement, named customers instead of "enterprise clients," a real market
size, and an ask sized down to two concrete time slots. Reporting at the
time described it as data-rich and low on drama, which is a strange thing to
say about a pitch that landed half a million dollars, until you notice that
drama is exactly what a triaging reader has no time for.

Allie Janoch's first email about Mapistry, by contrast, undersold the
product and went nowhere. Her second attempt, sent two years later, was
longer and led with substance instead of charm — and got a 33% reply rate
across investors. Length wasn't the variable. What changed was whether the
email gave the reader enough to decide with, and a longer email that answers
the triage question beats a shorter one that doesn't.

Dylan Field's early outreach for Figma wasn't a funding ask at all — he
emailed former colleagues from LinkedIn and Flipboard to find the product's
first users. It's a reminder that cold email covers more ground than
fundraising, and that the coldest emails that actually work often aren't
fully cold: they're aimed at someone who already has a thin, real reason to
care.

## The framing

In each case, the reader wasn't persuaded so much as given what they needed
to decide fast. That's the constraint this course treats as fixed: the
reader is triaging, with a few seconds and a stack of these to get through.
Everything covered from week 2 onward is a specific way writers fail that
constraint — a subject line that hides its own point, an ask too vague to
price, a length spent on the writer instead of the reader. None of them are
charisma problems.

## Exercise

Find one cold email in your own history — sent or received — that actually
worked. Write two sentences on what it gave the reader to decide with, and
whether that would still work if the email were twice as long.
