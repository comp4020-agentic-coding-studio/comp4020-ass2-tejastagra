The subject line gets a reader to open an email. What happens next usually
turns on the ask. This lecture argues that a vague ask doesn't fail because
it's rude or lazy — it fails because it moves work from the writer to the
reader, and the reader is the one person in the exchange who didn't agree
to do any work.

## Summary

"Would love to pick your brain sometime" asks the reader to do three jobs
the writer skipped: figure out what the meeting is actually for, estimate
how long it'll take, and decide whether that's worth their calendar. Each
of those is a small tax. Three small taxes, paid before the reader has even
decided whether they're interested, is usually enough to lose them. The ask
doesn't need to be small to work. It needs to be priced. "Fifteen minutes
on Thursday to see if X is worth building" costs the reader one decision,
not three, even though it's asking for exactly the same thing.

This is easy to miss because a vague ask often feels more polite to write.
"No pressure, whenever works" reads as considerate. To a reader triaging
fifty emails, it reads as an open-ended commitment with no shape, which is
harder to say yes to than a small, bounded one. Politeness and cost aren't
the same axis, and the mistake this week names is confusing them.

### A rubric worth applying to any ask

Three questions, in order:

1. Could the reader say yes to this in one sentence, without asking a
   clarifying question first?
2. Does the reader know roughly how much time or effort saying yes costs
   them?
3. Is there a specific next action, or does saying yes just open a longer
   negotiation?

An ask that fails any of these has moved cost onto the reader. Fixing it
usually isn't about adding politeness — it's about doing the pricing work
the writer skipped.

Composite example, illustrating the pattern:

Vague version:

> Would love to get your thoughts on what we're building whenever you have
> a moment — no rush at all!

Priced version:

> Fifteen minutes this week on whether the pricing model in [specific link]
> would work for a team your size — happy to send three times that suit
> you.

The second version answers all three rubric questions before the reader
has to ask them. It's not shorter out of virtue; it's shorter because
vagueness was doing the padding in the first one.
