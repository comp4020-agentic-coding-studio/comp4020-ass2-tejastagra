Every failure named so far costs a reply. This closing lecture covers one
that can cost a fine or a lawsuit, and ties the eleven before it into the
synthesis the final assessment asks for.

## Summary

Sending unsolicited commercial email without the disclosures the law
requires, harvesting addresses from a source you don't have permission to
email, or ignoring an unsubscribe request sit in a different category from
everything covered before this week — not "this got ignored" but "this was
illegal to send." It applies at the exact moment good craft turns into
volume, which is why it closes the semester rather than opening it: a
single well-crafted email rarely triggers it, a list of ten thousand
scraped addresses does.

Composite example, illustrating the pattern:

<div class="email-block">
  <div class="email-block__header">
    <p><strong>From:</strong> [Sender]</p>
    <p><strong>To:</strong> [Recipient]</p>
    <p><strong>Subject:</strong> Following up from [Conference]</p>
  </div>
  <div class="email-block__body">
    <p>Hi [Name], I got your details from the [Conference] attendee list and wanted to reach out — no unsubscribe link needed, just reply "no thanks" if this isn't relevant.</p>
  </div>
</div>

A specific ask, a real reason for reaching out, a short and well-structured
body, no manufactured urgency: this passes weeks 1 through 10 cleanly. The
address came from a conference attendee list the sender had no relationship
to and no consent to email, and "just reply no thanks" is not a lawful
unsubscribe mechanism. Good craft doesn't offset the absence of a lawful
basis to send it at all.

The synthesis point for the final assessment: a legally compliant email
that still fails weeks 1 through 11 is still a bad email, and a
well-crafted one that ignores this week is still a liability. Neither half
substitutes for the other.
