Last week's fix was pricing the ask. This week's failure sits earlier in
the email, in the line that's supposed to prove the writer did their
homework, and usually proves the opposite.

## Summary

"I loved your recent post on X" mentions the reader, but it isn't
personalisation in the sense that matters, because it could be sent to
anyone who wrote a post recently. Real specificity commits to a detail that
would be wrong for a different reader: a number from their actual work, a
decision they actually made, a consequence that follows from something only
they did.

The tell is almost always the second sentence. The first sentence can be
generated by a template with a name dropped in; the second sentence is
where a template has nothing left to say, and either goes vague or pivots
straight to the pitch.

Composite example, illustrating the pattern:

> I really enjoyed your recent talk on scaling engineering teams — it's
> clear you know this space inside and out. I wanted to reach out because I
> think our product could be a great fit for what you're building.

The first sentence could describe any talk by any engineering leader. The
second drops the reader entirely and pivots to a generic pitch. Real
specificity would have named what was actually said in the talk and
connected it, in one line, to something true only of this reader — a
detail that couldn't be swapped into an email to someone else without
becoming false.

### A test for the tell

Ask of any "personalised" line: would this sentence, word for word, also be
true of ten other recipients? If yes, it's not personalisation, whatever it
mentions by name. If a second sentence can't survive that test without
going vague, the personalisation was performed rather than real.

## Outline

- personalisation that survives being sent to anyone
- the second-sentence tell
- a test for telling performed personalisation from real specificity
- rewriting a generic personalisation line into a specific one
