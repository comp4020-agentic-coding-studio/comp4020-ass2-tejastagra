Last week's risk was the reader's. This week's is someone else's entirely —
a connector's — and the failure is writing the request as if that risk
didn't exist.

## Summary

Asking a stranger for their time and asking a connection to spend their own
credibility introducing you to someone else are different requests with
different costs. A cold ask risks the reader's ten minutes. A warm-intro
request risks the connector's relationship with the person they're
introducing you to — a cost most writers never mention, let alone address.

Writing both the same way — "would you be open to connecting me with X"
sent with the same effort as a cold email — treats the connector's risk as
if it weren't there. The fix is naming it explicitly: what you'll say to
the third party, why this specific introduction makes sense, and an easy
way for the connector to say no without it being awkward.

Composite example, illustrating the pattern:

<div class="email-block">
  <div class="email-block__header">
    <p><strong>From:</strong> [Sender]</p>
    <p><strong>To:</strong> [Connector]</p>
    <p><strong>Subject:</strong> Would you mind an intro?</p>
  </div>
  <div class="email-block__body">
    <p>Would you be open to connecting me with [Name]? I think what I'm working on could be really relevant to them.</p>
  </div>
</div>

This is addressed to the connector but reads identically to a cold email
addressed to the stranger — no mention of what the connector would say on
the sender's behalf, no acknowledgment that the introduction spends their
own relationship, and no easy way to decline. A version that treated the
request as its own genre would draft the introduction sentence for the
connector to copy, and offer an explicit out.

## Outline

- the connector's risk, and why most intro requests never mention it
- what a warm-intro request needs that a cold ask doesn't
- giving the connector a clean way to decline
