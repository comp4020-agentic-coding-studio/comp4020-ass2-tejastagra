## What this assignment tests

This assignment tests two things separately. First, whether you can
diagnose a cold email precisely, in your own words, pointing at the
specific line or structural choice causing the problem rather than
gesturing at "it's too pushy" or "it doesn't feel personal". Second,
whether you can actually repair one: turn that diagnosis into a redrafted
email that resolves it. Diagnosis without a working redraft is only half
the assignment. A redraft that happens to read better without addressing
what you diagnosed is not a pass either. The two have to line up.

This is deliberately not a template exercise. There is no fixed formula
you can apply to all ten emails and get a pass; each email fails for its
own reasons, and the redraft has to answer those reasons specifically.

## What you submit

Ten entries, one per email. Each entry must contain, in this order:

1. **The original email**, redacted, reproduced in full.
2. **A diagnosis** of what's wrong with it. For every problem you
   identify, point at the specific line or structural choice responsible,
   not a general impression of the email's tone or intent.
3. **A fix for each problem**, plus a brief justification of why that fix
   works, tied to something the course has taught (not just an assertion
   that it's "better").
4. **A full redraft** of the email that applies every fix from point 3 at
   once. The redraft must be a complete, sendable email, not a bullet list
   of changes or a partial rewrite of one sentence.

Submitted through Canvas under the Assignments page. There is no fixed
word count for the diagnosis or justification: write enough to make the
reasoning clear and stop. As a rough guide, most entries will run to a
few sentences of diagnosis and justification per problem identified, plus
the redrafted email itself.

## How to approach it

Read each email as its recipient would, in the first few seconds, before
you write anything down. The diagnosis should point at the email's own
words, not at cold email in general: quote or closely paraphrase the
specific phrase or structural choice you're objecting to. Before you
submit an entry, check the redraft against your own diagnosis line by
line. If you named three problems, the redraft needs to visibly fix all
three, not two of the three plus a general tidy-up.

## What a good submission looks like

One entry done well looks like this:

Email: "Hi, hope you're well. I'd love to pick your brain sometime about
your career path, would you be free for a quick call?"

Diagnosis: the second sentence asks for an open-ended call with no stated
topic or length, so the reader has to define the ask themselves before
they can even agree to it. There's also no reason given for why this
particular person was contacted, so the email reads as one of many
identical requests rather than something written for them specifically.

Fix and justification: name the specific question and cap the time. This
works because it moves the cost of defining the meeting from the reader
to the sender, which is the sender's job, not the reader's. Naming a
concrete reason for contacting this person addresses the second problem
by giving the reader something to weigh the request against.

Redraft: "Hi [name], I've been following your move from engineering into
product at [company] and I'm considering a similar shift. Would you have
15 minutes in the next couple of weeks to talk through how you made that
transition? Happy to work around your schedule."

That redraft resolves both problems named in the diagnosis: the ask is
now specific and time-boxed, and the reason for contacting this person is
stated rather than implied.

Compare that with a weak entry:

Email: same as above.

Diagnosis: "This email is too vague and doesn't really stand out."

Fix: "Make it more specific and personal."

Redraft: "Hi, I've read a lot about your career and would love to grab
15 minutes if you're free, no pressure either way."

This fails on all three parts. The diagnosis doesn't name what's vague or
where, the fix doesn't say what "more specific" means, and the redraft
still leaves the topic of the call undefined. It has the same structural
problem as the original with softer wording around it. A reader who
weighed this against the diagnosis above would see nothing in the
redraft that the diagnosis actually promised.
