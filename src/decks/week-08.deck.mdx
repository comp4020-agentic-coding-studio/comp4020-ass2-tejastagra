# The Warm Intro You Didn't Earn

A cold ask risks the reader's ten minutes. A warm-intro request risks
someone else's relationship entirely.

---

## Two different requests

Asking a stranger for their time costs the reader their own time.

Asking a connection to introduce you spends the connector's credibility
with the person they're introducing you to — a cost most writers never
mention, let alone address.

---

## The failure

Writing both the same way: "would you be open to connecting me with X,"
sent with the same effort as a cold email.

This treats the connector's risk as if it weren't there.

---

{/* _class: quote */}

<div class="email-block">
  <div class="email-block__header">
    <p><strong>From:</strong> [Sender]</p>
    <p><strong>To:</strong> [Connector]</p>
    <p><strong>Subject:</strong> Would you mind an intro?</p>
  </div>
  <div class="email-block__body">
    <p>Would you be open to connecting me with [Name]? I think what I'm working on could be really relevant to them.</p>
  </div>
</div>

Addressed to the connector, but reads identically to a cold email addressed
to the stranger. No mention of what the connector would say on the
sender's behalf. No easy way to decline.

---

## What the request actually needs

- what you'll say to the third party
- why this specific introduction makes sense
- an easy way for the connector to say no without it being awkward

Naming the connector's risk is the fix, not softer wording.

---

## Better version, same ask

Draft the actual introduction sentence for the connector to copy and
paste. Offer an explicit out: "no worries at all if this isn't a fit to
pass on."

---

## Recap

- a warm intro spends the connector's relationship, not just their time
- most intro requests never mention that cost
- naming it, and drafting the intro for them, is the whole fix

---

## Exercise

Take an intro request you've sent or received. Draft the sentence the
connector would actually forward. Add an explicit, low-friction decline.
