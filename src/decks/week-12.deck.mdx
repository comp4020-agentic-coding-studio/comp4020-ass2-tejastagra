# Week 12: Spam Law and Consent

Every failure named so far costs a reply. This one can cost a fine, or a
lawsuit.

---

## What this lecture covers

- why consent and disclosure sit in a different category from craft
- why this closes the semester rather than opening it
- a composite conference-list email that passes craft and fails law
- the closing synthesis: craft and compliance fail independently

---

## A different category

Sending unsolicited commercial email without required disclosures.
Harvesting addresses from a source you had no permission to email.
Ignoring an unsubscribe request.

These aren't "this got ignored." They're "this was illegal to send."

---

{/* _class: impact */}

Volume, not a single well-crafted email, is usually what triggers it.

---

## Why it closes the semester, not opens it

It applies at the exact moment good craft turns into volume. A single
well-crafted email rarely triggers it. A list of ten thousand scraped
addresses does.

---

A specific ask, a real reason for reaching out, a short, well-structured
body: this passes weeks 1 through 10 cleanly.

---

## Where it actually fails

The address came from a conference attendee list the sender had no
relationship to and no consent to email. "Just reply no thanks" is not a
lawful unsubscribe mechanism.

Good craft doesn't offset the absence of a lawful basis to send it at all.

---

## The synthesis point

A legally compliant email that still fails weeks 1 through 11 is still a
bad email.

A well-crafted one that ignores this week is still a liability.

Neither half substitutes for the other.

---

## What would have made it sendable

The same craft, sent only to people who opted in to hear from the sender,
with a working unsubscribe link that actually removes them.

The list is the whole fix. No rewrite of the body text changes whether the
sender had a lawful basis to send it.
