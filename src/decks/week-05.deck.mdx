# Length and Structure

A reader judges the shape of an email before they judge its content.

---

## Shape is information

Before reading a word, a reader sees: how long the paragraphs are, whether
there's a visible ask, whether it ends or trails into a signature.

Shape tells the reader how much of their time this will cost — before
content ever gets the chance to.

---

## The counterintuitive part

A long email organised around one clear ask reads faster than a short one
that rambles toward it.

Shorter, clearly structured emails tend to get read further and answered
faster than long, undifferentiated ones — worth testing on your own inbox,
not a word count to write to.

---

{/* _class: quote */}

Five paragraphs of company background, market context, and personal
introduction, before the actual request arrives as a single clause at the
end of paragraph four:

<div class="email-block">
  <div class="email-block__header">
    <p><strong>From:</strong> [Sender]</p>
    <p><strong>To:</strong> [Recipient]</p>
    <p><strong>Subject:</strong> Introduction and a bit about what we're building</p>
  </div>
  <div class="email-block__body">
    <p>...so if you had a few minutes to chat that would be amazing.</p>
  </div>
</div>

A reader skimming this sees an unbroken block with no visible ask — the
signal to stop, before the ask is even reached.

---

## The fix isn't cutting at random

Move the ask to where a skimming reader will actually see it.

Then cut whichever paragraph turns out not to change the reader's decision
if it's removed — usually most of them.

---

## Recap

- shape is read before content, independent of what the email says
- long-and-structured can beat short-and-rambling
- fix the ask's position first, then cut what doesn't move the decision

## Exercise

Take a long email you've sent. Find the sentence that actually asks for
something. Move it to the first two lines. See what else turns out to be
removable once it's there.
