# Week 1: Why Cold Email Works (At Least Sometimes)

Most cold email gets deleted unread. This deck is about the exceptions.

---

## What this lecture covers

- the claim: what a cold email has to do, before anything else
- three documented cases where it worked
- a prediction exercise on the first one
- what the three cases have in common
- how the next eleven weeks are organised

---

## The claim

A cold email lives or dies on whether the reader can decide about it in a
few seconds.

The reader owes the sender nothing, has fifty of these, and a few seconds
each. Every failure named this semester is a specific way of losing that
few seconds.

## Predict, then watch

Before the result: this pitch has no warm intro, no flattery, and no
urgency language. Talk to your neighbour: does it land, or get ignored?

---

## Case one: the result

Result: a $500,000 investment.

Coverage at the time described the pitch as data-rich and low on drama.
That's an odd thing to say about a message that landed half a million
dollars, until you notice that drama is exactly what a reader skimming
fifty of these has no time for.

## Case two: Janoch → investors

A first, short pitch for Mapistry went nowhere. A second attempt, sent two
years later, was longer and led with substance instead of charm.

<div class="email-block">
  <div class="email-block__header">
    <p><strong>From:</strong> Allie Janoch, Mapistry</p>
    <p><strong>To:</strong> [Investor]</p>
    <p><strong>Subject:</strong> Mapistry, two years on</p>
  </div>
  <div class="email-block__body">
    <p>Two years ago I sent you a shorter version of this and you passed. Here is what changed, and why the timing is different now.</p>
  </div>
</div>

---

## Case two: the result

Reported reply rate: 33% across the investors it went to.

Length wasn't the variable that changed. What changed was whether the
email gave the reader enough to decide with, and a longer email that does
that beats a shorter one that doesn't.

## Case three: Field's early Figma outreach

Not a funding ask. Dylan Field emailed former colleagues directly to find
Figma's first users: people who already had a thin, real reason to care.

<div class="email-block">
  <div class="email-block__header">
    <p><strong>From:</strong> Dylan Field</p>
    <p><strong>To:</strong> [Former colleague]</p>
    <p><strong>Subject:</strong> Trying something new: want an early look?</p>
  </div>
  <div class="email-block__body">
    <p>I'm building a design tool that runs in the browser. You came to mind from the work you were doing when we overlapped. Up for a look this week?</p>
  </div>
</div>

The coldest emails that work often aren't fully cold.

## What the three have in common

- the reader wasn't persuaded, they were given enough to decide fast
- none of the three lean on charm to do the work
- each prices exactly what it's asking the reader to do

---

## Recap

- the reader decided fast, under time pressure, rather than evaluating the
  pitch on its merits
- "enough to decide with, fast" is the bar every week after this one tests
  a specific way of failing
- eleven distinct failures follow, one per week, none repeating another
