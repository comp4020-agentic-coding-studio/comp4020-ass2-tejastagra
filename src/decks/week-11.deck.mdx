# Week 11: Automation and Scale

Week 4 argued for specificity that would be false for a different reader.
What happens to that argument at scale?

---

## What this lecture covers

- the trade automation makes for volume
- why a template can't hold week 4's kind of specificity
- a composite templated email and where it breaks
- the tell that gives a merged template away

---

## The trade

An automated sequence can send a thousand emails in the time a one-to-one
version takes to write one.

It cannot, by construction, contain week 4's specificity. That
specificity has to be true of one reader and false of the rest. A
sequence merges fields into a template; it doesn't know anything about the
recipient a variable didn't already know.

---

{/* _class: impact */}

Not an argument against automation. An argument for knowing what it costs.

---

## Not an argument against automation

It's an argument for knowing what it costs.

A sequence trades reply rate per email for volume of emails sent. A writer
who doesn't know that trade is happening will optimise the wrong thing.

## Predict, then watch

Before the next slide: predict which words in a merged-field template will
be the ones doing no real work. Then check.

---

{/* _class: quote */}

<div class="email-block">
  <div class="email-block__header">
    <p><strong>From:</strong> [Sender]</p>
    <p><strong>To:</strong> [Recipient]</p>
    <p><strong>Subject:</strong> Noticed what {"{{company}}"} is doing</p>
  </div>
  <div class="email-block__body">
    <p>I noticed {"{{company}}"} has been doing great things in the {"{{industry}}"} space lately.</p>
  </div>
</div>

Everything around this line can be competently merged. This can't: a
template holds a company name and an industry field, not what "great
things" means for one specific reader, so it falls back to a phrase true
of none of them in particular.

---

## The tell

Language a template falls back on when it has nothing specific left to
merge. Recognising it is the practical skill this week is for.

---

{/* _class: quote */}

<div class="email-block">
  <div class="email-block__header">
    <p><strong>From:</strong> [Sender]</p>
    <p><strong>To:</strong> [Recipient]</p>
    <p><strong>Subject:</strong> {"{{company}}"} and the {"{{industry}}"} problem</p>
  </div>
  <div class="email-block__body">
    <p>Sending this to a shortlist of {"{{industry}}"} teams we think are hitting [specific, real constraint]. If that's not {"{{company}}"}, ignore this. If it is, worth a reply.</p>
  </div>
</div>

Still a template, still merged at scale. It names the actual thing
segmenting the list, rather than inventing a compliment the merge fields
can't support.

---

{/* _class: centered */}

## Look this up

Find a templated email in your own inbox. Identify the merge fields.
Identify the sentence sitting next to them that's doing no real work:
that's the tell.

---

## Recap

- reply rate per email vs. volume of emails sent is the trade being made
- a template can't hold week 4's kind of specificity, only merge fields
- the tell: vague filler exactly where specificity should be
